Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Westland

Most premature tank failures start with skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are simple. Flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, have the anode rod inspected every few years, and test the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard to protect the heat exchanger.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the manufacture date, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is beyond that range and also shows discolored water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, inexpensive repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Westland

The majority of water heater failures we see in Westland start with the water itself. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. Once the sacrificial anode rod wears out, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless unit on hard water should get annual descaling. And an older installation being replaced often needs code items it never had: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
